Why I left the bench for a trading company, and what came with me

I left a career that produces knowledge for one that sits where knowledge is either used or lost. Some of what the laboratory taught me turned out to be the most useful thing I own, and some of it was no help at all.

Published 15 October 2026 6 min read Dr. Majjid A. Qaria

People who knew me in the laboratory ask the question with some care, as though I had left a religious order. People in trade ask it with curiosity, because a doctorate is not the usual entry ticket to a business that sells drums of powder and cartons of syringes. I have given both groups short answers for a while. This is the longer one, including the part I am least sure of.

What does research training give you in business?

Research training gives a habit of asking how a number was produced before trusting what it says, an instinct for what a specification leaves out, and comfort with saying what is not yet known. In pharmaceutical sourcing those habits apply directly to certificates of analysis and supplier claims. Negotiation, freight and contracts are learned separately.

I did not leave science because I stopped liking it. I left a particular position inside it, the one where you produce knowledge and hand it on, for a position further down the line, where the knowledge either arrives in a usable form or does not.

What the bench was

My first doctorate, at the University of Hyderabad, was on Helicobacter pylori and a modification it makes to cholesterol. The bacterium takes cholesterol from its host and attaches a sugar to it, and that glucosylated cholesterol sits in its cell wall and changes how the organism copes with antibiotics. The work was published in mBio in 2018. My second doctorate, at Harbin Medical University, moved from the bacterium to the patient's own cells: how a bacterial non-coding RNA suppresses the growth of lung cancer cells. After that came postdoctoral work at Jiangsu University.

Two doctorates in two countries on two quite different questions is not a straight path, and I do not pretend it was planned. What the two had in common was a habit of mind. In both, the interesting answer was rarely the headline molecule. It was what surrounded it, what modified it, and what stood between it and the place it needed to act.

Why I moved

The honest answer has more than one part, and I distrust anyone who gives a single reason for a decision of that size.

Part of it was distance. Research on antibiotic resistance is written in journals read by other researchers, and the resistance itself is shaped in places those journals rarely reach: in pharmacies where an antibiotic is sold by the strip, in warehouses without cooling, in markets where the only checking a medicine receives is whatever the buyer decides to do. I knew which end of that chain I was standing at, and it was the end furthest from the patient.

Part of it was a realisation about where errors live. In the laboratory, a bad reagent costs you a week and a repeated experiment. In supply, a bad batch does not announce itself, and nobody repeats the experiment. The person who finds out is the patient, and often they do not find out either. I came to think that the gap between a specification and a shipment needed people who could read both, and that there were not many of them in the trade I was looking at.

And part of it, I should say plainly, was that a business in Nanjing sourcing for buyers in the Middle East was something I could build, with what I had, in a country I already knew. Motives are rarely as clean as the story told about them afterwards.

What transferred

Less than people assume, and more than I expected.

Reading a method before reading a result

The most useful thing a doctorate taught me is to ask how a number was produced before I ask what it is. In a paper, the methods section is where you find out whether the result means anything. On a certificate of analysis, the equivalent is the method reference beside each result, the column most buyers skip. A purity figure from a method I cannot identify is a figure I cannot weigh, and I learned that at the bench long before I applied it to a drum of excipient.

Knowing what a specification leaves out

Researchers learn early that a reagent can meet its stated purity and still behave differently from the last lot. A specification lists what somebody chose to measure. It is silent on everything else. That instinct, that the gaps in a document are as informative as its contents, is the one I use most in sourcing.

Comfort with not knowing

Laboratory work is mostly not knowing, carefully. You hold a hypothesis loosely and design the next step to find out where it fails. Trade rewards confidence, and suppliers are generous with it. The research habit of saying that I do not know yet, and here is what would tell me, has been worth more in negotiation than any confident claim I could have made.

Respect for conditions

Working on a bacterium that survives in the stomach teaches you how much conditions change outcomes. Temperature, moisture, time and the material around a molecule all matter. That is most of what goes wrong with medicines between the factory and the shelf, and I recognised it as a familiar problem in a new setting.

What did not transfer

The research world runs on being right eventually. Trade runs on being right by a date. I had to learn that an answer which arrives after the shipment leaves is not an answer. I also had to learn that people do not owe you their reasoning. A reviewer must explain an objection. A supplier who declines to share a method owes me nothing, and my only lever is to buy elsewhere.

Negotiation, contracts, freight, payment terms, the ordinary mechanics of a trading business: none of that came from a laboratory, and I learned it the slow way, by doing it and getting parts of it wrong.

The other thing that did not transfer was the peer. In a laboratory there is always someone who will read your result with suspicion and tell you what is wrong with it. In trade that person mostly does not exist unless you hire them or become them.

Where the two meet now

NJMC sources active pharmaceutical ingredients, excipients and medical supplies. It does not manufacture. LNJC, the sister company, distributes in Yemen. The part of the work that looks most like my old life is reading documents with the suspicion I used to bring to a draft manuscript before it went out.

That habit is also why we built PharmaTrust. A user puts a certificate of analysis in front of it alongside the specification it claims to meet, and gets back a report of where the two line up and where something the specification asks for is missing or unclear. It reads documents, not medicines, and it does not replace a quality team or a laboratory. It asks the questions a careful reader would ask, so that a buyer without a laboratory background still gets to ask them.

What I am unsure of

I do not know whether I would have contributed more by staying. Research careers compound, and the work I did not do is invisible to me, so there is no way to weigh it. I think about that. I also think about the pharmacies at the other end, and about how few people who can read an analytical method are standing at the point where a purchase order is signed.

I left a career that produces knowledge for one that tries to make sure some of it is used. I cannot yet tell which of the two was the larger contribution, and I am wary of anyone who says they can about their own life.

Frequently asked questions

What does research training give you in business?

Research training gives a habit of asking how a number was produced before trusting what it says, an instinct for what a specification leaves out, and comfort with saying what is not yet known. In pharmaceutical sourcing those habits apply directly to certificates of analysis and supplier claims. Negotiation, freight and contracts are learned separately.

Is a PhD useful in pharmaceutical trading?

It is useful in a narrow and important way. It teaches you to read a method before a result and to notice what a document omits, which is most of the work of judging a certificate of analysis. It does not teach pricing, freight, contracts or how to run a company.

What did not transfer from research to business?

Timing and the peer. Research rewards being right eventually, and trade rewards being right before the shipment leaves. A laboratory always has someone reading your result with suspicion. In trade that reader mostly does not exist unless you hire one or become one.

Does NJMC manufacture medicines?

No. NJMC Medical Supplies, based in Nanjing, sources active pharmaceutical ingredients, excipients and medical supplies for buyers in Yemen, the wider Middle East and the United Kingdom. It sources, trades and consults, and does not manufacture. LNJC, the sister company, distributes in Yemen.
