What actually reaches a pharmacy shelf in a market under strain

Medicines in a strained market rarely arrive through a single door. The quality of each box depends less on the route it took than on how many people along the way asked a question about it.

Published 5 November 2026 7 min read Dr. Qaria

People who have never worked in a market like Yemen tend to imagine that medicines there arrive through one of two doors. Either a proper importer brings in registered products through a port, or smugglers bring in fakes. The reality on the shelf of an ordinary pharmacy is less tidy than that, and more interesting. Most of what is there arrived through a door that was open, legal in some respects and loose in others, and the quality of each box depends less on which door it used than on how many people along the way asked a question about it.

How do medicines reach markets with weak regulation?

In markets where regulation is under strain, medicines usually arrive through a mix of registered importers, aid procurement, regional wholesalers and repacked stock. The rules often remain, but the capacity to check every batch does not, so quality depends on the questions importers, distributors and pharmacists ask upstream, before goods move, rather than on any final inspection.

I have spent the years since I left the laboratory supplying ingredients and finished products into that kind of market, with LNJC, our sister company, distributing in Yemen. What follows is how I have come to understand the path a medicine takes to a shelf when the institutions that would normally check it are stretched, and where along that path the checking actually happens.

Strain removes capacity before it removes rules

A market under strain rarely loses its rules first. The registration requirements, the import permits and the labelling expectations usually survive on paper. What goes first is the capacity to apply them at the speed and scale that trade moves at. Inspection falls behind the volume of shipments, testing cannot reach every batch, and ports and border crossings work under pressures that have nothing to do with pharmacology.

So the useful description of such a market is not that it has no regulation. It is that checking has become optional in practice, and that the people who choose whether it happens are no longer mainly officials. They are importers, distributors, wholesalers and pharmacists, each deciding how much effort a box deserves before passing it on.

The routes a box takes

When formal channels are slow or expensive, other channels appear beside them, and a single shelf can hold products that came through all of them. Some arrive through registered importers with full documentation. Some arrive as donations or through aid procurement, which carry their own paperwork and their own assumptions. Some are bought from a wholesaler in a neighbouring country who bought them from another wholesaler, so that by the time they reach the pharmacist, nobody in the conversation has met the manufacturer. Some have been repacked, relabelled or split from larger packs along the way.

None of these routes is automatically bad. A product that took a long informal route can be perfectly good, and a product with a registration number can be poor. What the longer routes do reliably is lose information. Each handover is a place where the link between a box and its batch record can break, where storage conditions go unrecorded, and where a document can travel with goods it does not describe.

What a shelf selects for

A pharmacy shelf in a strained market is shaped by what can be seen. Price can be seen. Availability can be seen. Familiar packaging can be seen, and so can a brand name customers ask for. Quality, in the sense that matters to a patient, cannot be seen at all. A tablet with the right amount of active ingredient and a tablet with half of it look the same in the hand.

When the thing that matters is invisible and the things that do not matter much are visible, the market rewards the visible ones. A supplier who spends money on testing, proper storage and traceable sourcing carries costs that a pharmacist cannot see on the box, and will often lose a sale to one who does not. I do not say this as a complaint about pharmacists. They are making sensible choices with the information they have. The problem is the information.

Why this matters most for antibiotics

My first doctorate was on Helicobacter pylori, and on how it modifies cholesterol taken from its host to protect its cell wall and shape how it responds to antibiotics. Working on that question taught me how many ways a bacterium has of surviving a drug that was meant to kill it. It also left me with a particular fear about weak supply chains.

A counterfeit with no active ingredient fails one patient visibly: the infection does not improve, and someone eventually notices. A substandard antibiotic that delivers less than its labelled dose is worse in a quieter way. It treats some of the bacteria and leaves the hardier ones to grow back, which is very close to a description of how resistance is selected. The patient may partly recover. The failure is spread across a population and over time, where nobody can trace it to a box on a shelf.

The pharmacist is the last checkpoint and the least equipped

By the time a box reaches the pharmacy, the pharmacist is the last professional who handles it before a patient does. A careful pharmacist can do real work at that point: check the expiry date, look at the condition of the packaging, refuse a pack that has been opened or relabelled, ask the wholesaler where the stock came from, and store it properly once it arrives.

What a pharmacist cannot do is see inside the tablet. Assay, impurities, dissolution and microbial quality are laboratory questions, and they are almost never asked at the shelf. Everything the pharmacist can check is about the outside of the product and the honesty of the people who sold it.

Where the checking actually happens

In my experience, the meaningful checking in a strained market happens upstream, at the points where a buyer still has leverage: before an order is placed, before a shipment leaves, and at the first point of receipt. That is where a buyer can insist on knowing who made the product, ask for the certificate of analysis that belongs to the actual batch, ask how the goods will be stored and routed, and send samples to a laboratory when something does not add up. I have written separately about what a shipping route does to a medicine, because the journey is part of the product.

NJMC does not manufacture. We source, trade and advise, which means the questions we ask before goods move are, for some products, the most thorough questions anyone will ask about them.

Much of that upstream work is document reading, and it is where PharmaTrust fits. A user uploads a certificate of analysis with the specification it claims to meet and gets back a report of where the two agree and where the specification asks for something the certificate does not show. It analyses documents rather than medicines, and it does not replace a quality team or a laboratory. What it can do is make careful reading cheap enough that a distributor with a small team does it for every batch rather than for the ones that already look suspicious.

What I do not know

I do not know what share of the medicines on Yemeni shelves is substandard or falsified, and I am wary of anyone who gives a confident figure without saying how it was measured. Surveys of that kind are hard to run well in any market and harder in one under strain. I also do not know how the balance between formal and informal routes shifts from year to year, because by their nature the informal ones are not counted.

What I do know is narrower. Every question asked upstream removes some uncertainty that nobody downstream is in a position to remove. In a market where the patient has no way to check and the pharmacist has limited means to, that upstream question is often the only real test a medicine receives, and I would rather it be asked by someone who knows what the answer should look like.

Frequently asked questions

How do medicines reach markets with weak regulation?

In markets where regulation is under strain, medicines usually arrive through a mix of registered importers, aid procurement, regional wholesalers and repacked stock. The rules often remain, but the capacity to check every batch does not, so quality depends on the questions importers, distributors and pharmacists ask upstream, before goods move, rather than on any final inspection.

Is a medicine that arrived through an informal route necessarily poor quality?

No. A product that took a long informal route can be perfectly good, and a registered product can be poor. What longer routes reliably do is lose information: the link between a box and its batch record, the storage history and the match between documents and goods can each break at a handover.

Why are substandard antibiotics a particular concern in weak supply chains?

An antibiotic that delivers less than its labelled dose can treat some bacteria while leaving the hardier ones to grow back, which closely resembles how resistance is selected. Unlike an obvious fake, the failure is spread across many patients and over time, so it is rarely traced to the box that caused it.

What can a pharmacist check, and what can they not?

A pharmacist can check expiry dates and packaging condition, refuse opened or relabelled packs, ask where stock came from and store it properly. A pharmacist cannot see assay, impurities, dissolution or microbial quality, which are laboratory questions. Those checks have to happen upstream, before and at the first point of receipt.
