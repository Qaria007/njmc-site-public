Excipients are the part of a formulation nobody checks

Buyers ask hard questions about the active ingredient and almost none about the rest of the tablet. The rest is most of it, and it is where some of the worst failures in medicine have come from.

Published 29 October 2026 7 min read Dr. Majjid A. Qaria

When a buyer sends me a question about a tablet, the question is almost always about the active ingredient. Who makes it, what the certificate says, whether the assay is where it should be. It is the right first question. It is also, in most tablets, a question about a minority of the mass. The rest is excipient: the fillers, binders, disintegrants, lubricants, coatings, solvents and preservatives that turn a powder with a pharmacological effect into something a patient can swallow, store and trust to behave the same way every time.

Why do excipients matter to medicine quality?

Excipients make up most of a typical tablet or syrup and decide how it dissolves, flows, keeps and resists microbial growth. Many are also sold in food or industrial grades and change hands through traders, so the grade, the original manufacturer and an identity test on receipt matter as much as the active ingredient's paperwork.

Almost nobody outside a formulation laboratory asks about that rest. I have come to think this is the largest blind spot in the trade I work in, and the reasons for it are worth setting out, because none of them is stupidity.

Why attention goes to the active ingredient

The active ingredient is the thing named on the box. It carries the price, the patent history, the brand argument and most of the documentation a buyer is shown. When a supplier is assessed, the questions are framed around it. A buyer with limited time spends that time where everyone else is spending theirs, and that is why the gap persists.

Excipients, by contrast, are described in the language of inertness. They are called inactive ingredients on the leaflet. They are often familiar substances: starch, sugar, cellulose, talc, glycerin, water. Familiarity reads as safety. Nobody worries about starch.

Inactive is a claim about pharmacology, not about behaviour

An excipient is inactive in one narrow sense: it is not meant to produce the therapeutic effect. In every other sense it is doing work. The disintegrant decides how quickly a tablet breaks apart, and therefore how quickly the drug is available to dissolve. The lubricant decides whether the powder runs cleanly through the press, and too much of the wrong kind can slow dissolution. The coating decides how the tablet meets moisture and light. In a syrup, the solvent and the preservative decide whether the bottle is still fit to use weeks after it is opened in a warm house.

So when a generic behaves differently from the product it copies, the explanation is very often not the active ingredient. It is in the part of the formulation that the buyer's questions never reached.

My own research taught me to distrust the word inert. My first doctorate was on how Helicobacter pylori takes cholesterol from its host and modifies it, attaching a sugar, and how that modification helps protect its cell wall and shapes how it responds to antibiotics. Cholesterol is about as ordinary a molecule as biology has. To that bacterium it is a structural decision with consequences for survival. I learned there that a substance one discipline files as background, another discipline sees as the whole story. Excipients sit in exactly that position.

A substance name is not a grade

The practical problem in sourcing is that most excipients are not made only for medicines. The same substance name is produced for food, for cosmetics and for industrial use, sometimes in the same plant, often traded through the same chemical distributors. What separates a pharmaceutical grade from the others is not the name on the drum. It is the specification the material is made and tested against, the controls on how it is made, and the record of where it went between the plant and the buyer.

That last part matters more for excipients than for most active ingredients, because excipients change hands more. A drum may be bought by a trader, repacked into smaller containers, relabelled and sold on. Each step is a place where a technical grade can be presented as a pharmaceutical one, where a certificate from one lot can travel with material from another, or where a substitution can happen because the right material was short and something with the same name was available.

The worst known excipient failures in the history of medicine have followed exactly this pattern. Glycerin and propylene glycol, two of the most common solvents in oral liquids, have more than once been replaced by or contaminated with diethylene glycol, an industrial chemical that is toxic to the kidneys. The finished syrups went to children. The episodes have recurred across decades and in different countries, and the mechanism each time was not exotic chemistry. It was a solvent that nobody tested for identity because it was only a solvent.

What I look at in an excipient's paperwork

None of this calls for exotic analysis. It calls for asking the same questions of the minority ingredients that buyers already ask of the major one.

First, the grade. I want the certificate and the label to state which grade the material is and which specification it is tested against. A certificate that lists results without naming the standard behind them is a list of numbers, not a statement about fitness for a medicine.

Second, who made it, as distinct from who sold it. If the seller is a trader or a repacker, I want the name of the original manufacturer and the original certificate, and I want to see that the lot numbers connect. When they do not connect, I have learned to stop the conversation there rather than accept an explanation.

Third, identity. For excipients where a substitution would be dangerous, and the liquid solvents are the obvious case, the specification should include tests that would detect the known substitutes, and someone downstream should actually run an identity test on receipt rather than trusting the label. That is a laboratory's job, not a document reviewer's, and it is the one step that would have stopped the poisonings.

Fourth, the microbiology. This is where my training makes me more nervous than most buyers. Many excipients come from plants, animals or fermentation: starches, celluloses, lactose, gelatin, natural gums. They carry something of their origin with them, including moisture and microbial load, and how they are dried, stored and packed decides whether that load stays where it should. A syrup base is, to a microorganism, a meal with water in it. The preservative system is all that stands between that meal and growth, and the preservative is an excipient too.

Where software helps and where it stops

Most of the reading above is document work, and it is tedious in the way that invites shortcuts. That is part of why we built PharmaTrust at NJMC. A user uploads a certificate of analysis together with the specification it claims to meet, and gets back a report of where the two line up and where the specification asks for something the certificate does not carry. It treats an excipient certificate exactly as it treats one for an active ingredient, which is the point. It analyses documents rather than medicines, and it does not replace a quality team or a laboratory. It can make sure the paper has been read all the way through, including the parts nobody usually reads.

What I do not know

I cannot tell you how often excipient problems reach patients in the markets I supply. I do not believe anyone has counted, in Yemen or in most of the places LNJC and our buyers distribute into, and I am not going to put a number on something that has not been measured. What I can say is that in my own work the excipient questions are the ones most often answered slowly, vaguely or not at all, and that a question answered vaguely is usually a question nobody upstream asked either.

NJMC does not manufacture. We source, trade and advise, which means the questions we ask are the only checking some of these materials receive before they enter a formulation somewhere else. I would rather ask about the starch and be thought fussy than not ask and find out later what was in it.

Frequently asked questions

Why do excipients matter to medicine quality?

Excipients make up most of a typical tablet or syrup and decide how it dissolves, flows, keeps and resists microbial growth. Many are also sold in food or industrial grades and change hands through traders, so the grade, the original manufacturer and an identity test on receipt matter as much as the active ingredient's paperwork.

What is the difference between a pharmaceutical grade excipient and a technical grade one?

The substance name can be identical. What differs is the specification the material is made and tested against, the controls on how it is made, and the record of where it travelled before reaching the buyer. A certificate that does not name the grade and the standard it was tested against does not show which one you have.

Why are liquid solvents such as glycerin singled out?

Because they have repeatedly been replaced by or contaminated with diethylene glycol, an industrial chemical that is toxic to the kidneys, and the resulting syrups reached children. An identity test on receipt that can detect the known substitutes is the step that would have caught those cases, and it needs a laboratory, not only a document review.

Can a certificate of analysis alone show that an excipient is safe to use?

No. A certificate is a claim about a lot, made by whoever issued it. Reading it carefully shows whether the right grade, standard and tests are named and whether the lot connects to the original manufacturer. Whether the drum holds what the paper says is a question only testing answers.
