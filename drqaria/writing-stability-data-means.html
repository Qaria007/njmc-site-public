What stability data promises, and the conditions that void the promise

The date on a box of medicine is a conclusion from experiments run under particular conditions, in particular packaging. Outside those conditions the date is still printed, but the evidence behind it has stopped applying.

Published 12 November 2026 7 min read Dr. Qaria

Every box of medicine carries a date after which it should not be used, and most people read that date as a property of the medicine, like its colour or its strength. It is not quite that. A shelf life is a conclusion drawn from a set of experiments, and like any experimental conclusion it holds only under the conditions the experiment was run in. Outside those conditions the date is still printed on the box, but the evidence behind it has stopped applying.

What does a shelf life actually guarantee?

A shelf life promises that batches like this one, in the same packaging and stored under the conditions on the label, were tested over time and stayed within specification for that period. It promises nothing once the product has been exposed to heat, humidity, light or freezing, or moved into packaging that was never studied.

I came to pharmaceutical trade from two research careers, and the habit both of them left me with is to ask, of any claim, what was actually measured and under what conditions. When I apply that habit to an expiry date, the answer is more specific and more fragile than the printed date suggests.

A shelf life is a conclusion, not a property

A manufacturer does not discover a shelf life by waiting to see when a product goes bad. It places batches of the product, in the packaging it will be sold in, under defined storage conditions, and tests them at intervals. Some batches are held under the conditions the label will recommend. Others are held under harsher conditions, warmer and more humid, so that changes which would take a long time appear sooner. From the pattern of results, the manufacturer proposes a period over which the product is expected to stay within its specification, and the expiry date is that period added to the date of manufacture.

Put plainly, the date on the box says this: batches like this one, packed like this one and stored like this one, were tested over time and stayed within their limits for at least this long. Every part of that sentence carries weight. Change the packing or the storage and the sentence no longer describes the box in your hand.

What the studies actually look at

A stability programme does not simply ask whether the active ingredient is still there. It asks several questions at each time point. Is the assay still within its limits? Have degradation products appeared, and are they below the levels the specification allows? For a tablet or capsule, does it still dissolve the way it should, so that the ingredient that is present actually becomes available to the patient? Has the appearance changed? For products where it matters, has the microbial quality held, and has the preservative kept working?

This is the part I most want buyers to understand. A medicine can lose its quality without losing much of its active ingredient. A tablet that has absorbed moisture can still contain its full labelled dose and release it more slowly than it should. A solution can hold its assay while a degradation product grows that the original testing at release never had reason to look for. The expiry date speaks for all of these attributes together, and only under the conditions in which they were all measured.

The packaging is part of the claim

The studies are run on the product in its final container, because the container is doing much of the protective work. Each container was chosen to keep out moisture, light or air, and the stability data describe the product inside that barrier.

This is why repacking worries me more than almost anything else in a loose supply chain. When tablets are taken out of their original blisters and put into a different container, or a large pack is split into smaller ones in a warehouse, the product is now in packaging that was never studied. It may be fine. Nobody has shown that it is, and the expiry date still printed on the outer carton was not earned in the new container.

What voids the promise

The conditions that break the link between the date and the evidence are mostly ordinary. Heat is the obvious one, and it does not need to be dramatic: a container left on a quay, a truck parked in the sun, a storeroom with no cooling in summer. Humidity matters as much for many solid products, and it does its work quietly. Light degrades some ingredients. Freezing ruins some products that were only ever meant to be kept cool, and an emulsion or a suspension that has frozen may look nearly normal once it thaws.

Then there are the handling failures: a damaged blister, a bottle left open, a seal broken for inspection and not replaced. Each of these puts the product outside the barrier its data describe. I have written separately about what a long shipping route does to a medicine, because the journey is often where the conditions on the label stop being met, and nobody downstream can see that it happened.

Why this matters most for antibiotics

My first doctorate was on how Helicobacter pylori modifies cholesterol taken from its host to protect its cell wall and shape how it responds to antibiotics. That work left me with a strong sense of how much a bacterium can survive when the drug it meets is weaker than intended. An antibiotic that has partly degraded in storage does not fail in an obvious way. It delivers less than the prescriber meant, treats some of the infection, and leaves the hardier organisms behind, which is the pattern I described in an earlier piece on resistance and supply chains. A stability failure in an antibiotic is a clinical problem for one patient and a resistance problem for everyone else.

What I ask for as a buyer

NJMC does not manufacture. We source, trade and advise, so stability data reach me as documents, and I read them with a few questions in mind. Does the storage statement on the label match the conditions the stability studies were run under? Is the product being supplied in the same packaging that was studied, or has it been repacked somewhere along the way? For an active ingredient, is there a retest date, and do I know how the material has been stored since it was last tested? How will the goods be stored and routed between the supplier and our receipt, and will there be any record of the temperature on the way?

The certificate of analysis for a batch shows that the batch met its specification on the day it was tested. It says nothing about what happened afterwards. I wrote about what a certificate of analysis does not say before, and storage history is one of the largest gaps. Part of that reading can be made faster. It analyses documents rather than medicines, and it does not replace a quality team or a laboratory. It cannot tell anyone whether a drum sat in the sun.

What I do not know

I do not know how much of the medicine that reaches strained markets has been stored outside its labelled conditions at some point, and I do not trust anyone who claims to know without describing how they measured it. Nor can I say, for a particular product that has been mishandled, how much quality it has lost. That depends on the ingredient, the formulation, the packaging and the exposure, and the only honest answer comes from testing that product.

What I can say is that a shelf life is a promise with conditions attached, and those conditions are printed on the box beside the date. A buyer who reads only the date is reading half of the promise. The other half, about how the product was kept, is what strained supply chains break most often, and the damage is invisible: a box that sat in a hot container for a week looks the same as one that did not.

Frequently asked questions

What does a shelf life actually guarantee?

A shelf life promises that batches like this one, in the same packaging and stored under the conditions on the label, were tested over time and stayed within specification for that period. It promises nothing once the product has been exposed to heat, humidity, light or freezing, or moved into packaging that was never studied.

Is a medicine safe to use right up to its expiry date if it was stored badly?

Not necessarily. The expiry date was set from studies of the product stored under the conditions on its label, in its original packaging. If it was exposed to heat, humidity, light or freezing, or repacked, the evidence behind the date no longer applies, and only testing can say how much quality was lost.

Why does repacking matter for stability?

Stability studies are run on the product in its final container, because the container protects it from moisture, light and air. Once a product is moved into packaging that was never studied, nobody has shown that it will stay within its specification until the printed date, even if it often does.

Does a certificate of analysis show that a batch has been stored correctly?

No. A certificate of analysis shows that a batch met its specification on the day it was tested. It says nothing about the conditions the batch was kept in afterwards. Storage history has to come from temperature records and from the people who handled the goods.
