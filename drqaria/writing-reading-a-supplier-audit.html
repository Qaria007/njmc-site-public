Reading a supplier audit report as a scientist rather than a buyer

An audit report is offered as reassurance and usually filed as one. I read it as a sample of a factory taken on particular days, and most of what it means sits in the pages buyers skip.

Published 22 October 2026 7 min read Dr. Majjid A. Qaria

A supplier audit report usually arrives as an attachment to a sales conversation. It is offered as reassurance, and most buyers read it that way: they look at the cover page, check the name of the site, glance at the number of findings and file it. I read it the way I used to read the methods section of a paper I was asked to review, which is slowly, and with more interest in what was done than in what was concluded.

What should you look for in a supplier audit?

Read a supplier audit as a sample rather than a verdict. Check the scope first, and whether the product you buy was made in an area the auditors covered. Separate what was observed from what was shown or told, weigh the kind of finding over the count, and treat corrective actions as promises until evidence follows.

That is not cynicism about auditors. Most are careful people working against the clock. It is a recognition of what an audit is: a sample, taken by particular people, on particular days, of a much larger reality that keeps running after they leave.

An audit is a sample, not a census

In the laboratory, nobody trusts a result without knowing how the sample was drawn. If you test one colony you know something about that colony. Whether you know anything about the plate depends on how you chose it. An audit has the same structure. Auditors spend a limited time at a site. They walk some areas and not others, read some batch records and not others, and interview the people who are made available to them. Everything in the report is a statement about that sample.

So the first pages I look for are not the findings. They are the pages that describe the sample: the dates, the names or roles of the auditors, the areas visited, the documents reviewed and the scope statement. A report that does not tell me these things is like a paper without a methods section. It may be correct, and I have no way to know.

Scope is where most of the meaning sits

The scope statement says what the audit was about. It is often one or two sentences, and buyers skip it most often.

A manufacturer may run several buildings, several production lines and several product families on one site. An audit can cover the whole site or a part of it. It can be organised around a quality system in general or around a named product. The material I am buying may be made in a workshop the auditors walked through, a workshop they saw from the corridor, or a workshop the report does not mention at all. A clean report on the finished dosage building tells me very little about the intermediate made across the yard.

When I read a scope statement I ask one question: is the thing I am buying inside it? If I cannot answer that from the document, I ask the supplier, and I ask for the answer in writing. A surprising number of conversations end there.

Observed, told and shown

Audit findings are written in a flat, neutral register, which hides an important difference between kinds of evidence. Some statements describe what the auditor saw: a room, a gauge, a log being filled in. Some describe a document the auditor was shown. Some describe what the auditor was told by someone at the site. All three belong in a report. They are not equally strong.

In research we would call these direct observation, secondary record and self report, and we would weigh them differently. I try to do the same here. A sentence like "the procedure requires" tells me what a document says. A sentence like "records reviewed showed" tells me something was checked against the document. Where a report is written mostly in the first form, I read it as a description of a system on paper rather than of the system in operation.

The number of findings is not the result

Buyers like a count. Few findings feels good, many findings feels bad, and none at all feels best. I think that instinct is often the wrong way round.

A complex site examined over a short visit will almost always show something worth writing down. When a report on such a site records nothing, I do not conclude that nothing was wrong. I conclude that I need to understand why nothing was recorded. The sample may have been narrow. The visit may have been short. The auditor may have been told in advance what to look at. In an experiment, a result with no variation at all is the one that makes me check the instrument.

What interests me more than the count is the character of the findings. A finding about a missing signature on a form is a different kind of thing from a finding about how a deviation was investigated, or how a cleaning step was shown to work. The first is housekeeping. The second tells me how the organisation thinks when something goes wrong, which is the thing I most want to know and the thing an audit is least able to show in a day or two.

A corrective action is a promise until it is evidence

Most reports end with a list of findings and the responses to them. The responses are usually written by the site, and they are usually reasonable. They describe what will be changed and by when. What they often do not include is evidence that the change happened and worked.

I read that section the way I read the discussion of a paper that announces future experiments. The intention is useful information. It is not a result. If the report I am given is old enough that the promised dates have passed, the natural next question is what the follow up showed, and whether there was one.

Date, and what has happened since

An audit describes a site at a moment. Staff change, equipment is replaced, a product moves to another line. None of it appears in a report written before it happened. I do not have a rule for how old a report can be before I stop relying on it, and I am wary of anyone who offers one without explaining where it comes from. What I do is ask what has changed at the site since the audit, and pay attention to how readily the question is answered.

What an audit cannot tell me

An audit is evidence about a system. It is not evidence about a batch. A well run site can still release a lot that is wrong, and a site with weaknesses can still produce a lot that is fine. The batch I receive is described by its certificate of analysis and, where it matters, by testing I commission. NJMC sources and trades; it does not manufacture, so for us the audit and the certificate do different jobs and neither can stand in for the other.

The tool we built, PharmaTrust, works on the certificate side of that line. A user gives it a certificate of analysis and the specification it claims to meet, and gets back a report of where they line up and what is missing or unclear. It does not read audit reports and it does not inspect sites. That part still needs a person reading carefully.

What I am unsure of

I cannot visit every site we buy from, and I would be overstating my own reach if I pretended otherwise. Much of the time an audit report written by someone else is the best view I will get of a factory. Reading it as a sample does not make it more complete. It only makes me clearer about where its edges are, and more specific in what I ask next.

I also do not know how often the reports I have read reflected an ordinary day at the site and how often they reflected a prepared one. The report alone cannot tell me, and I would rather say so than let a tidy document answer a question it never could.

Frequently asked questions

What should you look for in a supplier audit?

Read a supplier audit as a sample rather than a verdict. Check the scope first, and whether the product you buy was made in an area the auditors covered. Separate what was observed from what was shown or told, weigh the kind of finding over the count, and treat corrective actions as promises until evidence follows.

What should you check first in a supplier audit report?

Check the scope statement and the description of the visit before the findings: the dates, who audited, which buildings and lines were seen, and which documents were reviewed. The key question is whether the product you are buying was made in an area the audit actually covered. If the report cannot answer that, ask the supplier in writing.

Does an audit report with no findings mean the supplier is good?

Not on its own. A complex site examined in a short visit usually shows something worth recording, so a report with no findings is a reason to look at how narrow the sample was, how long the visit lasted and who chose what was inspected. The type of finding says more than the count.

Can an audit replace a certificate of analysis or batch testing?

No. An audit is evidence about how a site runs its systems. It says nothing direct about a particular batch. The batch is described by its certificate of analysis and, where it matters, by independent testing. The two documents answer different questions and neither substitutes for the other.
